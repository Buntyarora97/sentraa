What Is an Ophthalmologist — and Why Does the Difference Matter?

When you search for an "ophthalmologist near me," it helps to understand exactly what that term means — and why it is different from an optometrist or an optician. An ophthalmologist is a medical doctor who has completed medical school (MBBS), followed by a postgraduate degree in ophthalmology (MS or DNB), and often additional fellowship training in a specific subspecialty such as retina, cornea, glaucoma, or paediatric ophthalmology. An ophthalmologist can diagnose all eye diseases, prescribe medications, perform eye surgery, and manage complex medical eye conditions.

An optometrist, by contrast, typically holds a Bachelor of Optometry (B.Optom) degree and specialises in vision testing, prescribing glasses and contact lenses, and detecting certain eye abnormalities. An optician fits and dispenses glasses and lenses based on a prescription. Neither optometrists nor opticians perform surgery or treat complex medical eye conditions like diabetic retinopathy, retinal detachment, or advanced glaucoma.

This distinction matters because many patients first visit an optician for new glasses, only to discover later that their blurred vision was caused by cataract, diabetes-related retinal damage, or another underlying medical condition that an optician is not trained to detect. Seeing a qualified ophthalmologist from the start ensures that serious eye conditions are caught early — when treatment is most effective and vision loss is still preventable.

Comprehensive vision testing is just the beginning — a full ophthalmic exam goes far beyond prescribing glasses.

What to Expect at Your Ophthalmologist Consultation

If this is your first visit to an ophthalmologist, here is what your consultation at Sentra Clinic will involve:

  1. Registration and history: We record your medical history, current medications, and any eye complaints. Bring your old glasses and any previous eye reports.
  2. Vision testing: Your visual acuity is checked — how well you see at distance and near, with and without your current glasses.
  3. Refraction: If needed, the technician determines your exact glasses prescription using an auto-refractor and trial lenses.
  4. Eye pressure check: A quick, painless test (applanation tonometry) to screen for glaucoma.
  5. Slit-lamp examination: The surgeon examines the front of your eye — lids, cornea, iris, and lens — under high magnification using a slit lamp.
  6. Dilated fundus examination: Dilating drops are used to widen your pupil so the surgeon can examine the retina, macula, and optic nerve in detail.
  7. Advanced diagnostics (if needed): OCT, fundus photography, topography, or perimetry may be performed on the same visit.
  8. Discussion and plan: The surgeon explains the findings, answers your questions, and recommends a treatment plan — all in your preferred language.

Frequently Asked Questions About Seeing an Ophthalmologist

What is the difference between an ophthalmologist and an optometrist?

An ophthalmologist is a medical doctor (MBBS + MS/DNB) who can diagnose all eye diseases, prescribe medications, perform surgery, and manage complex medical eye conditions. An optometrist (B.Optom) specialises in vision testing, prescribing glasses and contact lenses, and detecting certain eye abnormalities but cannot perform surgery. For any medical eye condition — cataract, glaucoma, retinal disease — you need an ophthalmologist.

How often should I see an ophthalmologist for a routine checkup?

Adults with no eye problems should have a comprehensive eye exam every 1-2 years. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of eye disease, annual checkups are recommended. Children should have their first eye exam by age 3, and annually thereafter if any refractive error or squint is detected.

Will my eyes be dilated during the exam?

For a comprehensive eye exam — especially if you are over 40 or have diabetes — we typically use dilating drops to examine the retina thoroughly. Dilation blurs near vision for 2-3 hours, so we recommend bringing sunglasses and arranging for someone to drive you home. Not all visits require dilation; the surgeon will decide based on your condition.

How long does an ophthalmologist appointment take?

A comprehensive eye exam typically takes 60-90 minutes, including registration, preliminary testing, surgeon consultation, and any advanced diagnostics. If dilation is needed, allow an additional 30-45 minutes for the drops to take effect. Follow-up visits are usually shorter, around 30-45 minutes.
